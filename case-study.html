National Gallery of Art — Case study

Turning a wrong guess into a reason to visit

ArtSwipe · Lead Designer · 10 weeks · 2024

Scroll

TL;DR

A swipe-to-sort attribution game where a wrong guess becomes a link to a visit, not a dead end.

NGA needed a game that grew engagement on nga.gov and got people through the museum doors. I designed ArtSwipe — cards appear one at a time, you swipe toward the artist you think made the work, and getting it wrong opens the artwork's NGA page, flagged if it's currently on view.

75% of usability testers preferred it over Artle, NGA's existing game, across 9 within-subjects tests I proposed to capture preference and playability in one session. The metric that mattered most — did this actually get people into the gallery — was never instrumented. I named that gap at handoff instead of projecting a number I didn't have.

Originated and pitched the ArtSwipe concept, selected by NGA for development. Led the reframe of the brief from a DAU target to a retention-and-footfall problem. Ran competitive analysis and usability testing on NGA's existing game to set design constraints. Designed the interaction model and mid-fi prototypes, proposed and ran the testing methodology, and delivered the concept directly to NGA stakeholders. Partnered with our researcher on the data schema underpinning the game.

The real stakes

"Build another game" was the stated brief. The gap it left was the actual one.

NGA's site pulled 16.9M visits in 2023, more than doubling in two years. Fun Seekers — people looking for interactive content — were named the target audience, 57% of all users were on mobile, and most Fun Seekers were already playing Artle daily. What that habit didn't do: connect to the gallery itself, or to learning anything durable about the art.

The reframe

DAU is the result, not the lever.

I reframed the brief around retention mechanics instead of first-session engagement, because a game that's fun once doesn't move daily numbers — a game people mention to a friend does. That's the Wordle model: the social loop is the distribution channel.

Discovery — what Artle told us

Artle's problem wasn't difficulty. It was that difficulty was never legible.

Feedback came too late to course-correct, and the interaction rewarded a correct outcome, never the attempt. I ran a competitive analysis and moderated usability tests on Artle before sketching anything, and turned three findings into non-negotiable constraints: clear signifiers of how a player was doing mid-session; feedback delayed instead of instant, so players couldn't cheat; and an interaction that rewards the act of guessing — Artle's players were reverse-image-searching answers because the game gave them nothing else to hold onto.

User interviews sharpened the target: people play casual art games on a subway ride or a lunch break, not like a 100-hour RPG. Sessions had to be short by design, competition had to be personal, and the job-to-be-done wasn't "learn about art" — it was "feel less clueless about art, without feeling stupid." That last point came straight from the room:

"I feel nothing because I just looked up the image."Jesse, on playing Artle
"Makes me feel stupid or unaware… this looks like it's for people with an art-history background."Rhea, on playing Artle

Two different failure modes — indifference and exclusion — same root cause: the game gave players no way in.

Building within NGA's real constraints

A browser game with limited backend support — not a funded app build.

That ruled out anything flashier than it needed to be: no 3D, no AR/VR, no GPS mechanics tying gameplay to a spot in the gallery. Every interaction had to run on a lightweight frontend with minimal backend load.

The constraint didn't just eliminate options — it pointed toward the answer. A mechanic already in muscle memory, needing zero custom infrastructure to feel good, was both the UX-right and the engineering-realistic choice.

Concept — why ArtSwipe

One mechanic, three problems solved.

Our 4-person team split into two working groups, each developing a concept in parallel. ArtSwipe was mine — pitched, refined, and selected by NGA for development. Across both groups we brainstormed 8 concepts, all constrained to NGA's existing collection: everything from an "Art Heist" narrative game to a 3D gallery map.

ArtSwipe won because swiping is already in muscle memory from dating apps, so onboarding cost is near zero; attributing art to an artist forces you to notice style, not guess randomly, so the "guess" is quietly educational; and the host picking a theme before a card appears loads the social hook before the first swipe.

Single-player or multiplayer via a shared link, no account required. The host picks a theme — Women Artists, Black Artists, Latinx Artists, LGBTQ+ Artists, or Randomize — and one of two difficulty levels, deliberately just two: more granularity creates decision fatigue at the exact moment you need someone to just start playing.

Design decisions

Mid-fi first, on purpose — not a shortcut.

It surfaced structural problems before we spent time on visual polish.

IssueCauseFix
Players anticipated one card, blindsided by the stackNo depth cueDeck shown with visible depth — sets correct anticipation upfront
Feedback gave away answers, invited cheatingResult shown instantlyDelayed reveal, visual pause state

9 within-subjects tests, Artle vs. ArtSwipe back to back — my proposed method, and the source of the 75% number.

Trade-offCall
Difficulty namingResearch showed players felt "stupid," not challenged. Default level: Normal, not "Easy." Hard strips artist name and style notes. Flagged by NGA's UX designer as an accessibility win.
Collection scopeThe mechanic needs artists with a strong visual signature. It doesn't scale to a one-painting artist — an open question NGA raised themselves.
Content-ops dependencyThe gallery link only stays honest if NGA keeps "on view" tags current. Out of scope here — flagged at handoff, not hidden.
Invite drop-offInviting a friend means leaving the browser tab. No response in 3 minutes → auto-prompt into single-player, so the loop doesn't stall.

Systems thinking — the data model

The interaction and the data it depends on, designed at the same time.

The end-screen link only works if the underlying data can support it. I partnered with our researcher to define the schema behind it — not in sequence, in parallel.

Scale risk

Multiplayer works because it's friction-by-design. That friction disappears the moment the game gets popular.

A link from someone you know, not a public lobby — no room verification, no identity layer. A link leaking into a public forum is the likely failure mode once reach grows, not an edge case. I flagged room-access controls as a v2 priority.

Outcome

75% of testers preferred ArtSwipe over Artle.

"We thought a lot about ArtSwipe — this was a novel approach, something we never thought of."Martin Franzini, Chief of Digital Product & Experience, NGA
"I really appreciate the nuances you were thinking of — with the indicators, we are big on accessibility."Deanna Wood, UX Designer, NGA
"Can't believe you did this in the amount of time you had."Memo Saenz, Product Manager, NGA

What I didn't claim. Gallery-visit attribution — the metric that actually mattered — needs in-app behavior connected to physical entry data. That instrumentation sat outside this engagement's scope. Projecting a number for it would have been more comfortable than admitting I didn't have one. I named the metric, named why it wasn't tracked, and left it there.

Reflections

  1. Reframing the brief is the job. The OKR said "grow engagement." The real brief was a retention problem and a footfall problem — naming that early shaped every decision after it.

  2. Test the logic before the pixels. All three critical issues at mid-fi were structural, not visual. Hi-fi would have hidden them until they were expensive to fix.

  3. Design decisions carry backend cost. The most meaningful feature was also the one with the longest-lived dependency — flagging that at handoff is part of the job.

  4. Don't project metrics you didn't measure. 75% and three stakeholder quotes are real. Knowing which number I couldn't give you is a stronger signal than making one up.
